"The number is even **or** it is odd." A statement joined by *or* claims that at least one of two things holds. Logicians write `A ∨ B`. In mathematics *or* always allows both: "A or B" is true when A holds, when B holds, and when both do.

## Proving an or

To prove "A or B" it is enough to prove one of them, and you must say which. The move **left** commits to proving A; the move **right** commits to proving B.

```

Choose badly and you are stuck with a goal you cannot prove. That is what **Undo** is for. Here only one side can be done.

## Using an or: proof by cases

Knowing "A or B" is weaker than knowing an and: you know one of them holds, but not which. So to use it you must be ready for both. *"Either A holds or B does. In the first case… In the second case…"* This is **proof by cases**, and it is everywhere in mathematics.

The move is **obtain** again, but look at what it writes this time: `obtain hP | hQ := h`. A bar `|` between the names, in place of the brackets and comma. The bar means "or": two **cases**, and so two goals. In the first you have `hP`, in the second `hQ`. Lean labels the cases `inl` and `inr`, for the left and the right side of the or. The goal is the same in both, and you must reach it twice.

```

When the goal is itself an or, each case can choose its own side.

## What you used

- `left` or `right` to prove `A ∨ B`: choose which side to prove.
- `obtain hA | hB := h` to use `h : A ∨ B`: two cases, one with `hA : A` and one with `hB : B`, and the goal to be shown in each.
