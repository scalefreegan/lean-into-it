There is a statement called **False**. It has no proof, ever. It is the dead end of logic, and it turns out to be very useful.

"Not A", written `¬A`, means: **if A were true, False would follow**. To Lean `¬A` is exactly the if-then `A → False`, and nothing else. So you already know how to work with it: the moves for if-then are the moves for not.

## Using a not

Since `hnP : ¬P` is really `P → False`, you can **apply** it whenever the goal is False. The goal becomes P.

```exercise
id: clash
```

Having both P and ¬P among your facts is a **contradiction**. You have just shown what that means: False follows.

## Proving a not

To prove `¬P`, prove `P → False`: **intro** assumes P, and the goal becomes False. Your job is then to find a contradiction.

This exercise is an old pattern of argument: if P would give Q, and Q fails, then P fails.

```exercise
id: prove-not
```

## From a contradiction, anything follows

Suppose your facts contradict each other and the goal is some unrelated Q. Is Q proved? Yes. A proof of Q has to work in every situation where the facts hold, and there is no such situation. Mathematicians say the statement is true *vacuously*.

The move **exfalso** makes this step: it replaces any goal by False. Use it when you can see a contradiction among your facts.

```exercise
id: anything
```

When two facts are flatly each other's negation, the move **contradiction** does all of that in one line: Lean looks through the facts for such a pair itself.

```exercise
id: at-once
```

## Proof by contradiction

So far the goal False was either given, or came from a goal that was a not. **Proof by contradiction** uses the same idea for any goal: *"Suppose the goal were false. Then… which is absurd. So the goal is true."* The move is **by_contra**: it adds "the goal is false" to your facts, and the goal becomes False.

```exercise
id: not-not
```

That proof, that "not not P" gives P, cannot be done with the earlier moves alone. It needs a principle the others do not: that every statement is either true or false, the **law of excluded middle**. Logic that assumes it is called *classical*, and it is the logic of ordinary mathematics. Logic without it is called *constructive*, and it has its own uses, mostly where a proof should also be a way of computing something.

Lean keeps track. It can report the axioms a proof rests on, and this app asks for that report after every proof. For the proof you just made, where Lean has no way of working out whether P holds, the report lists one called `Classical.choice`, the axiom Lean gets excluded middle from. This app accepts it: it is one of the three standard axioms all of Mathlib is built on.

A rule of thumb: prove things directly when you can, and reach for `by_contra` when the goal gives you nothing to work with.

## What you used

- `¬A` means `A → False`. So `intro hA` proves a not, and `apply h` uses `h : ¬A` when the goal is False.
- `exfalso` replaces any goal by False.
- `contradiction` finishes any goal when two facts are `A` and `¬A`.
- `by_contra h` assumes the goal is false, calls that `h`, and makes the goal False.
